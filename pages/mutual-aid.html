Solidarity in Practice

Mutual Aid

Not charity. Not pity. A recognition that our fates are bound together.

Mutual aid is one of the oldest human practices. Long before there were states, churches, or NGOs, communities survived by sharing — food, labor, knowledge, protection, care. This is not radical. It's what humans do when they're not convinced that their neighbors are their enemies.

Mutual aid is not a stopgap. It is the model. It is how life actually works when systems aren't designed to extract from it.

What Mutual Aid Is

Mutual aid means people getting together to meet each other's needs with the explicit understanding that the need flows in all directions. The food bank volunteer also has needs. The person receiving groceries also has skills and time to offer. The exchange is not transactional — it builds relationship, trust, and collective capacity.

Real mutual aid networks:

  • Are non-hierarchical — no caseworkers, no gatekeeping, no means-testing
  • Recognize everyone as having something to give and something to receive
  • Build ongoing relationships, not one-time transactions
  • Are transparent about resources and decision-making
  • Politicize participation — they help people connect personal struggle to structural causes

What Mutual Aid Is Not

Charity is not mutual aid. Charity maintains the status quo by managing its worst effects. It depends on the goodwill of the comfortable and preserves the hierarchy between giver and receiver. Mutual aid challenges the conditions that create need.

Government social services are not mutual aid. They may sometimes do good, but they are gatekept, conditional, often punitive, and designed to manage populations rather than build community power.

Historical Examples

Mutual aid is not new and it is not utopian. It has always existed, especially in communities that have been excluded from state support:

  • Freedmen's Bureau alternatives — Freed Black Americans built their own schools, hospitals, and mutual aid societies when the state abandoned them after the Civil War.
  • Black Panther Party survival programs — Free breakfast programs, health clinics, legal aid, and more. These programs were targeted by the FBI specifically because they were effective.
  • ACT UP and the AIDS crisis — Communities devastated by AIDS organized their own care networks when governments ignored them. They also forced policy change through collective action.
  • Anarchist collectives in 1930s Spain — Workers and farmers ran their own factories and farms collectively, providing for millions of people.
  • COVID-19 mutual aid networks — Thousands of local networks emerged globally in 2020, providing food, medicine delivery, childcare, and more — often reaching people no institution could.

How to Find or Start a Network

If a network already exists in your area, join it. If it doesn't, start one. Starting is easier than it seems.

  • Begin with a needs/offers inventory in your neighborhood or social network
  • Create a simple shared document: who needs what, who can offer what
  • Make one match — connect one person who has something to one person who needs it
  • Repeat

The infrastructure grows from the relationships. Start with the relationships. A network of eight people who genuinely show up for each other is more powerful than a thousand-person organization that exists primarily on social media.

Looking for existing networks? Search for mutual aid groups in your city on social media, community boards, and neighborhood apps. In many cities, networks built during COVID-19 still exist and are active. Ask around — these networks often operate word-of-mouth by design.
