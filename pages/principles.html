What We Stand For

Principles

Not a manifesto. Not dogma. Working principles for people trying to build something real.

1. We Share More Than We're Told

The most powerful political act available to the ruling class is convincing ordinary people that other ordinary people are their primary threat. This has been the playbook for centuries. Recognize it. Refuse it. Build trust across the lines you're told to fear.

2. Mutual Aid Over Charity

Charity maintains hierarchy: the giver is elevated, the receiver is diminished. Mutual aid refuses that transaction. In mutual aid, we help each other because we understand that our wellbeing is linked — that when you're okay, I'm more okay, and vice versa. Solidarity is not a favor. It's a strategy.

3. Community Self-Organization Works

Don't wait for institutions to save you. They were not built to save you. History is full of communities that organized themselves — tenant unions, food co-ops, community land trusts, rotating credit associations, mutual aid networks. These things work. Build them where you are.

4. Every Person Has Something to Contribute

Skills, time, knowledge, labor, care, space, connections — everyone has something. The myth of meritocracy tells us that some people are fundamentally more valuable than others. This is a lie designed to sort us. The ability to cook, to fix, to listen, to organize, to carry, to translate: these are all essential. They are not ranked.

5. Borders Divide What Solidarity Can Unite

National borders are tools of state power. They determine who gets rights, who gets resources, who gets to move freely, and who gets stopped. The worker in your country and the worker outside it are not natural adversaries — they're natural allies being kept apart by the same forces that exploit both of them. Act across those lines whenever you can.

6. Conflict Is Real, But Division Is Manufactured

Not all conflict is manufactured. Real injustices exist; real power disparities cause real harm. But manufactured conflict — the culture war, the race panic, the immigration scare — is deliberately produced to redirect anger away from its actual sources. Learn to tell the difference. Direct your energy accordingly.

7. Small Actions Are Not Small

Helping your neighbor is political. Sharing food is political. Building trust across difference is political. You do not need to go to every protest, run for every office, or shout the loudest. The quiet, persistent work of showing up for people around you is the foundation everything else is built on.

8. This Is Not Utopian

Expecting people to cooperate for mutual survival is not idealism — it's realism. Humans cooperated for most of their history. The societies built on pure competition are recent, and they are visibly failing. Mutual aid is not a dream. It is the actually-existing alternative that communities around the world practice every day.

9. Power Concedes Nothing Without Demand

Frederick Douglass said it. It remains true. Building community power is not just about survival — it's about creating the conditions under which better things become possible. Organize. Build. Push. The demand for a more just world is not naive; it's necessary.

10. No One Is Free Until All Are Free

This is not a slogan. It's a description of how interdependence actually works. A world where some people are disposable is a world that will eventually find reasons to dispose of more people. The boundary of who counts moves. Build a world that doesn't have that boundary.
